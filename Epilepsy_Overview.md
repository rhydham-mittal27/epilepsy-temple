# Epilepsy — Complete Overview

*As of 2026-09-27. Compiled from WHO, ILAE, AAN and peer-reviewed sources (listed at the end).*

---

## 1. What it is

- Epilepsy is a long-term brain disease that causes **recurrent, unprovoked seizures**.
- A **seizure** is a burst of abnormal, overly synchronised electrical activity in the brain. The core problem is an imbalance between excitation (the chemical messenger **glutamate**) and inhibition (the chemical messenger **GABA**).
- **Formal definition (ILAE 2014).** Any one of these counts as epilepsy:
  1. Two or more unprovoked (or reflex) seizures more than 24 hours apart.
  2. One unprovoked seizure plus a **60% or higher chance** of another within 10 years, for example when the EEG or MRI shows an abnormality.
  3. A diagnosed epilepsy syndrome.
- **When it counts as "resolved":** the person has been seizure-free for 10 years and off medication for 5, or has outgrown an age-dependent syndrome.
- A single *provoked* seizure is **not** epilepsy. Examples are seizures from fever, low blood sugar or alcohol withdrawal.

## 2. How common it is

| Measure | Figure |
|---|---|
| People affected worldwide | ~**50 million** |
| New diagnoses per year | ~5 million |
| Active epilepsy | 4–10 per 1,000 people |
| New cases per year | 49 per 100,000 in high-income countries; up to 139 per 100,000 in low/middle-income countries |
| Share living in low/middle-income countries | ~80% |
| India | About **1 in 6 of all cases worldwide** (~8–10 million). Prevalence 2.5–11.9 per 1,000, roughly **2× higher in rural areas** (11.9 vs 5.7) |
| Untreated people in India (treatment gap) | **50–90%** |
| Untreated people in low-income countries | ~75% |
| Risk of early death | Up to **3×** the general population |

## 3. Causes

ILAE groups causes into **six categories**. About **50% of cases have no identifiable cause**.

| Category | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Structural** | Hippocampal sclerosis (scarring in a memory region; the most common cause of drug-resistant temporal lobe epilepsy), stroke, head injury, brain tumours, malformations of the brain's outer layer, birth injury or lack of oxygen at birth |
| **Genetic** | *SCN1A* (about 80% of mutations cause Dravet syndrome), *SCN2A*, *KCNQ2*, *CDKL5*, *STXBP1*; also inherited generalised epilepsies such as juvenile myoclonic epilepsy and childhood absence epilepsy |
| **Infectious** | **Neurocysticercosis** (pork tapeworm cysts in the brain), which accounts for ~1 million epilepsy cases in India and is the top cause of acquired epilepsy in children; also meningitis, encephalitis, TB, HIV, cerebral malaria |
| **Metabolic** | Pyridoxine (vitamin B6) dependency, GLUT1 deficiency, mitochondrial disease |
| **Immune** | Autoimmune encephalitis (anti-NMDA receptor, LGI1, GAD65 antibodies) |
| **Unknown** | ~50% of cases |

**Neurocysticercosis in India:** in epilepsy cohorts it has been found in 11–61% of patients, depending on region and setting. In Assam studies, 42–61% of active epilepsy cases met the diagnostic criteria.

**Common triggers in people who already have epilepsy:**

- **Missed medication** (the single most common trigger)
- Sleep deprivation
- Alcohol, including binge drinking and withdrawal
- Stress
- Fever or illness
- Menstrual hormone changes ("catamenial" seizures)
- Flashing lights, which affect only about 3–5% of people with epilepsy

## 4. Seizure types and symptoms (ILAE 2025 classification)

The 2025 update:

- cut the list from 63 seizure types to 21
- dropped the word "onset" from the class names
- uses consciousness (awareness plus responsiveness) as the key classifier
- renamed "motor vs non-motor" to "observable vs non-observable"
- added "epileptic negative myoclonus" as a new type

There are four classes: **focal, generalised, unknown, and unclassified**.

### Focal (starts in one area of the brain)

- **With preserved consciousness** (previously called an "aura"):
  - Déjà vu, fear, or a rising feeling in the stomach
  - Strange smells or tastes
  - Tingling
  - Twitching of one limb
  - Visual flashes
- **With impaired consciousness:**
  - Blank stare
  - Automatic movements such as lip-smacking, chewing or fumbling
  - No memory of the event afterwards
  - Lasts 30 seconds to 2 minutes
- **Focal to bilateral tonic-clonic:** spreads to both sides of the brain and becomes a full convulsion.

### Generalised (both sides of the brain from the start)

| Type | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| Tonic-clonic ("grand mal") | Stiffening, a cry, a fall, rhythmic jerking, tongue biting, incontinence, then confusion and sleepiness |
| Absence ("petit mal") | 5–15 second blank stare, sometimes with eyelid fluttering; mostly in children; can happen dozens of times a day |
| Myoclonic | Sudden, brief muscle jerks, often in the morning (typical of juvenile myoclonic epilepsy) |
| Atonic | Sudden loss of muscle tone, a "drop attack" with a risk of injury |
| Tonic | Stiffening only, often during sleep |
| Clonic | Rhythmic jerking only |
| Epileptic spasms | Clusters of flexing or extending movements in infants (West syndrome) |

### After a seizure (postictal phase)

- Confusion, headache, sleepiness
- Temporary weakness on one side (Todd's paralysis)
- Memory gaps

## 5. Diagnosis

1. **A detailed eyewitness account or smartphone video.** This is the most valuable diagnostic tool.
2. **Routine EEG** (recording of the brain's electrical activity):
   - The first EEG catches epileptic spikes only **~40–50%** of the time.
   - Repeated EEGs raise this to 82–92%.
   - A 24-hour ambulatory EEG catches them in about 72% of cases.
   - Sleep deprivation, recording during sleep, hyperventilation and flashing-light tests all increase the chance of catching spikes.
3. **MRI with an epilepsy protocol** (1 mm slices). ILAE recommends this for everyone at diagnosis.
4. **Video-EEG monitoring** is the gold standard. It separates epilepsy from its mimics:
   - Fainting (syncope)
   - Functional/psychogenic (non-epileptic) seizures
   - Heart arrhythmias
   - Migraine
   - Sleep disorders
5. **Other tests:** blood work (glucose, sodium, calcium), genetic panels, ECG, and a lumbar puncture if infection is suspected.

## 6. Treatment

### Medications (antiseizure medications)

- About **70%** of people can become seizure-free with the right medication.
- Seizure-free rates by drug attempt (Kwan & Brodie):
  - 1st drug: ~47–50%
  - 2nd drug: an extra ~12%
  - 3rd drug: an extra ~4%
- **Drug-resistant epilepsy** means two well-chosen, tolerated medications have failed. It affects about **1 in 3 patients**.
- **Common drugs:**
  - Broad-spectrum: levetiracetam, valproate, lamotrigine
  - Mainly for focal seizures: carbamazepine and oxcarbazepine
  - Also used: lacosamide, topiramate, brivaracetam
  - **Cenobamate** is strong in drug-resistant focal epilepsy.
- **Syndrome-specific drugs:**
  - Fenfluramine and cannabidiol for Dravet and Lennox-Gastaut syndromes
  - Ganaxolone for CDKL5 disorder
  - Everolimus for tuberous sclerosis
- **Pipeline:** **azetukalner (XEN1101)** works on Kv7.2/7.3 potassium channels, and Xenon planned to file for US approval in 2026. Others in trials include BHV-7000, soticlestat and gene therapies.
- **Valproate warning:** exposure in pregnancy causes birth defects in about **10%** of babies and neurodevelopmental problems in **30–40%**. It's avoided in women who could become pregnant.

### Surgery and devices for drug-resistant epilepsy

| Option | Outcome |
|---|---|
| Anterior temporal lobectomy (removing part of the temporal lobe) | **60–80% seizure-free**; risk to memory and language |
| Laser ablation (MRgLITT, heat-destroying the seizure focus through a small hole) | ~49–56% seizure-free, with faster recovery |
| Vagus nerve stimulation (VNS), responsive neurostimulation (RNS), deep brain stimulation (DBS) | Reduce seizures (often by half or more in responders) but rarely stop them; considered palliative |
| Ketogenic / modified Atkins diet | Seizures halved in about 50% of children; in adults, 13% become seizure-free and 53% have at least a 50% reduction |

## 7. Emergencies and risks

- **Status epilepticus** is a seizure lasting **5 minutes or more**, or repeated seizures without recovery in between.
  - Give a benzodiazepine: midazolam into the muscle or nose, or lorazepam or diazepam into a vein.
  - Brain damage builds up after about 30 minutes.
- **Seizure first aid:**
  1. Time the seizure.
  2. Put the person on their side and cushion their head.
  3. Put **nothing** in their mouth.
  4. Don't hold them down.
  5. Call an ambulance if it lasts more than 5 minutes, if the person is injured or pregnant, if a second seizure follows, or if it's their first seizure.
- **SUDEP (Sudden Unexpected Death in Epilepsy):**
  - Rates: about 1.2 per 1,000 adults per year and 0.22 per 1,000 children per year.
  - Three or more tonic-clonic seizures a year raise the risk **15-fold**, up to about 18 per 1,000 per year.
  - Other risk factors: nighttime seizures, being alone, lying face-down, and not taking medication.
  - **Supervision at night and seizure alarms reduce risk.**
- **Other conditions that often come with it:**
  - Depression in ~39% and anxiety in ~36% of people with epilepsy
  - Memory and cognitive problems
  - Injuries, stigma and driving restrictions

## 8. Wearables and physiology (relevance to Temple)

### Cleared seizure-detection devices

| Device | Where worn | Signals used |
|---|---|---|
| Empatica Embrace2 / EpiMonitor (~$399) | Wrist | Motion, electrodermal activity (skin conductance), temperature |
| EpiWatch | Apple Watch app | Motion and heart rate |
| NightWatch | Upper arm, at night | Heart rate and motion |
| UNEEG SubQ, Epiminder Minder | Under the scalp | EEG over months to years |

- Embrace2 caught all 16 tonic-clonic seizures in one study, with a median delay of 25 seconds.
- **Every non-EEG wearable is good only at convulsive seizures.** Focal seizures with impaired consciousness, and absence seizures, are mostly missed.

### Physiology signals a Temple-style optical sensor *could* in theory pick up

- **Heart rate during a seizure:**
  - It rises by more than 35 bpm or 55% in many seizures.
  - That happens in about 51% of children's seizures and is more common in focal seizures, seizures longer than a minute, and seizures during sleep.
  - One heart-rate-variability algorithm reached **84.8% sensitivity** with 0.25 false alarms a day.
- **Blood flow during a seizure:** the brain gets **more blood** on the side where the seizure starts. NIRS (near-infrared light shone through the skull) detected this in **96%** of cases, versus 69% for ictal SPECT (a nuclear-medicine blood-flow scan).
- **Seizure forecasting:**
  - Most patients have individual **multi-day seizure cycles**, which can be tracked with heart-rate wearables and under-scalp EEG.
  - Cycle tracking beat simple moving-average forecasts in about 86% (hourly) and 63% (daily) of comparisons.

### Implication (inference, not an established finding)

A temple-worn device that combines optical sensing (heart rate plus a brain-blood-flow proxy) with **EEG electrodes** would fill a real gap: detecting non-convulsive and focal seizures, and forecasting risk. No current product has been shown to do this.
